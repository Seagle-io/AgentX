The Mona Lisa,
and a smile that comes and goes.

Leonardo da Vinci, 1503-1519. A portrait does not roll like a wave and has no spark to send across. What it has is that smile which appears when you look elsewhere. A page cannot know where you are looking — so the painting oscillates instead.

How it is made

The smile is a low frequency.

The Mona Lisa's smile is said to fade when you stare at it. The explanation lies in the eye: peripheral vision picks up mostly low spatial frequencies, where the shadow at the corners of the mouth reads as a smile; central vision, far sharper, no longer sees it. Look at the eyes and she smiles; look at the mouth and she does not.

01

Making the painting oscillate

A web page does not know where you are looking, and there is no question of teaching it. So we do the opposite: the shadow at both corners of the mouth deepens then fades, on an eleven-second cycle. The smile comes and goes on its own. Faster and it flickers; slower and you never notice it.

02

Two spots, not a mouth

The effect touches only two small Gaussian spots, placed on the corners measured straight off the image. Their radius is expressed in fractions of height — otherwise the shadow spreads into an oval on a portrait format — and beyond a certain radius you are no longer deepening a smile, you are staining a cheek.

03

Sfumato and craquelure

Two other layers, almost imperceptible: a very slow veil modulating the mid-tones, and the drift of the cracks in the varnish. As everywhere else, only the phase depends on time; the sine and cosine of each phase are precomputed per cell.

04

The framing

The painting is portrait, a hero frame is landscape. On a wide screen the portrait is shown almost whole, centred, with the wall darkening around it — closing in on the face made it enormous and unreadable. On a phone, whose format is close to the painting's, it fits nearly entirely.

Choosing the characters

Sixteen steps from empty to solid.

On a dark ground, a dense character reads as a light area. Density and colour therefore say the same thing. The wash under each cell is heavier here than elsewhere: measured, the painting rendered at 62 of mean lightness against 118 in the source, and the face was drowning in the dress.
